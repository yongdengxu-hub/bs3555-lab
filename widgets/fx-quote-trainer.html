Every year, exam Q1 hands you two primary quotes against a vehicle currency and a direct cross quote, and asks: invert them, build the synthetic cross (worst-case pairing: bid÷ask one way, ask÷bid the other), then compare the two bands. No overlap = arbitrage. Overlap but different = shopping around. This trainer deals you fresh quotes forever; work each one on paper, then reveal.

Why this matters — real examples

Cross-rate arithmetic is the entry test of every FX desk, and the "bands must overlap" logic polices $9.6 trillion of daily trading.

Financial marketsTriangular arbitrage now lives for milliseconds

Academic studies of EBS tick data found triangular arbitrage opportunities in EUR/USD/JPY lasting under a second, harvested almost entirely by algorithms. The BIS 2025 survey puts the dollar on one side of 89% of all trades precisely because everyone routes through the deepest market, exactly like your synthetic leg.

→ The link: the "no overlap" verdicts you just classified are what those algorithms check, thousands of times a second.

Monetary policyThe sterling flash crash, 7 October 2016

At 7:07am Hong Kong time, GBP/USD fell about 9% in 40 seconds, and different venues printed different prices for the same pound: textbook arbitrage that nobody could execute because liquidity had vanished. The BIS post-mortem is a case study in what happens when the trainer's tidy bands break down.

→ The link: arbitrage enforces the law of one price only when someone can actually trade both legs.

Your moneyThe airport kiosk is a 5–10% spread

The interbank GBP/USD spread you see here is a few pips (about 0.02%). Airport bureaux routinely quote spreads of 5–10%: the same mathematics, a thousand times the margin. Once you can read a two-way quote, you can never be surprised by "0% commission" again.

How to use it in class

Deal a set of quotes, give the room three minutes on paper (invert, synthesise, classify), then reveal. The verdict sentence, who trades where and why, is where the 13 marks live in exam Q1(c).
