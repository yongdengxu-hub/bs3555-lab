Why this matters — real examples

Garman–Kohlhagen (1983) is Black–Scholes with two interest rates: still the quoting language of a market that trades about $700bn of FX options every day.

Financial marketsDesks quote vol, not price

Ask a dealer for a three-month euro call and the answer is "7.6": an implied volatility, which this formula converts to money. The smile (out-of-the-money puts commanding higher vols) is the market's confession that lognormality understates crashes, exactly what the CHF de-peg of January 2015 proved in forty minutes.

→ The link: your σ slider is the number that desks actually negotiate.

Monetary policyRisk reversals called Brexit early

Days before the June 2016 vote, the premium of GBP puts over GBP calls (the risk reversal) hit record levels while the spot barely moved: the options market pricing referendum risk in advance. Central banks monitor these numbers as forward-looking risk sensors.

→ The link: price the same option once with σ = 8 and once with σ = 20 and you have quantified fear.

CareersN(d₁) is a job description

An options desk's daily life is holding N(d₁)/(1+r*) of the underlying against every option written, rebalancing as it moves: delta hedging. The 1997 Nobel prize (Merton and Scholes) was for showing that this recipe, not a forecast, is what an option price is.
