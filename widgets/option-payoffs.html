Every option strategy is just kinked lines added together. Build up to three legs (each a call, put or forward, long or short, with its own strike and premium) and read two lines: payoff (at expiry, ignoring what you paid) and profit (payoff minus premiums). The presets rebuild the module's stars: the straddle that printed money on referendum night, and the KIKO structure that ruined exporters.

Why this matters — real examples

Payoff diagrams are the grammar of every structured product ever sold, honest or otherwise.

MacroReferendum night, June 2016

On 22 June 2016 cable traded at 1.48 and a one-month straddle cost about 4 cents. Friday morning: 1.32. The straddle paid roughly 12 cents net without requiring any view on the result, only the view that 1.48 would not survive the night. Overnight implied volatility had reached about 50%: the market pricing a coin flip.

→ The link: load the straddle preset and read its breakevens: that is exactly the bet.

Financial marketsKIKO, Korea 2008: "zero cost" wasn't

Exporters funded the puts they wanted by writing calls, often on twice the notional. When the won collapsed the short calls knocked in and losses were unbounded; an estimated $3bn+ vanished and litigation ran for years. The payoff line tells you instantly what the salesman didn't.

→ The link: load the KIKO preset and look left and right: floor on one side, cliff on the other.

Your moneyStructured deposits in the bank branch

"Capital-protected FX-linked bonds" sold at retail are a deposit plus a bought (or, in bad versions, sold) option. Drawing the product's payoff before signing is the single most useful consumer-finance skill this module teaches.

→ The link: any product whose brochure can't be drawn with three of these legs is hiding a fourth.
